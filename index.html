What the speed figure is, exactly. It is an effort estimation by the designers themselves: every one of the 37 sized the same four tasks twice — how big this job was before AI, and how big it is today — and the figure is the ratio between the two. It is not a stopwatch and not a time-tracking log. That has one consequence worth stating plainly: people tend to remember the old way as slower than it was, so if this number is wrong, it is far more likely to be too generous than too harsh.
🟠 Why “2× on tasks” is not “2× on the week” Estimated
1
The 2× was measured on hands-on work. The four tasks we sized are all making — designing, specifying, synthesising, testing. The measurement says nothing about the rest of the calendar.
2
About 40% of the week isn't hands-on. Meetings, syncs, reviews, coordination. The survey puts meetings & calls alone at roughly a third of the week — and AI removes none of it. You cannot prompt your way out of a meeting.
3
So only part of the week compresses. The hands-on 60 halves to about 32; the untouched 40 stays 40. The same week of work now costs about 72 instead of 100.
→ Across a full week, “twice as fast on tasks” is worth about 1.4×. And that is before the new deliverables — prototypes and code — arrive to fill the time it freed.

01 So where did the saved time go?

Being twice as fast does not give you twice the free time. The saving was quietly absorbed — and by the one kind of work that never shows up in a productivity number.

1
Work nobody used to ask us for
Completely invisible in the “twice as fast” number

The four tasks behind the 2× compare like with like: work that existed before AI and still exists today. That is exactly what makes the measurement fair — and also what makes it incomplete. It cannot see work that had no “before”.

A clickable prototype is now the single most expected thing a designer hands over — level with the spec document and ahead of a plain Figma file. Two years ago, asking most of the team for that would have been unreasonable. And when we asked people what they can now make that they couldn't before, 22 of the 37 answers said “prototypes”.

How we know: we simply counted. We asked everyone what their PM expects when a design task is done, and counted the answers. Prototypes: 22 people out of 37. No estimating involved.
What is a judgement: calling those deliverables “new”. The survey never asked “is this new?” — we classified them as new because they have no pre-AI equivalent. That part is our reading, and we flag it as such. Our reading
This is the win and the hidden cost, at the same time. The team didn't just get faster — it got bigger. Work that used to belong to engineering, or simply never happened at all, now belongs to UX. That is a real expansion of what design owns. It also means a large part of the time we saved was spent the moment we earned it, on work that no before-and-after comparison will ever be able to show.

About 29% less effort than before AI — at best. Not the 50% that “twice as fast” would suggest. And “or more” matters: the last step is real work we cannot yet put a number on, so the true figure can only move in one direction — up.
The one thing worth taking away: at today's speed the team could absorb roughly 40% more tasks before it is working as hard as it did before AI. Nobody's workload went down. Not one designer in 37 says they take on fewer tasks than before AI, and 91% say they take on more. That is the other half of the speed story told in the team's own voice: the work did not shrink to fit the time AI freed — it grew to fill it. ⏱ The final number — how occupied is a designer today?

The ladder above counts task effort. To answer “how busy are we, really?” you have to put the whole week back together: the meetings that never compressed, the tasks that halved, and the third-more of them we now take on.

≈ 7 hrs
freed per designer per week — everything counted except the brand-new work. The week that used to cost 100 now costs about 83; on a 40-hour week that is ~6.9 hours. Estimated
And the survey confirms it from the other side. Asked directly — “how many hours per week does AI save you?” — the team's blended answer is ~7.1 hours (⏳ Hours Saved, Report tab). A chain of estimates and a direct measurement landing 0.2 hours apart is the strongest cross-check on this page: the two routes share no data, and they arrive at the same place. Measured
So why doesn't the week feel 7 hours lighter? Because those hours are consumed as fast as they appear — by the deliverables that didn't exist before (59% are now expected to hand over an HTML prototype, 11% production code), and by developers who are themselves about twice as fast and therefore consume design content at roughly double the old rate. Counting both, a designer today is effectively as occupied as before AI. What changed is not how full the week is — it is what comes out of it: close to double the output, plus kinds of output design never produced before. That is why “AI saves me 7 hours” and “I have never been busier” are both true at once. Our reading
If you only remember one sentence: the team got about twice as fast at the work it already had — and then spent most of that gain on more work, on entirely new work that didn't exist before, and on keeping up with developers who got faster at exactly the same time. That is why doubling our speed feels like almost nothing at all: the ~7 hours a week AI frees are spent the moment they appear.

01 Who counts as a champion — and who doesn't
🎯 The four tests Our reading

Every one of the 37 uses AI, and 95% use it daily — so frequency selects nobody. These seven were picked because their open answers showed all four of the following. It is a judgement call on our part, not a survey ranking.

1
Depth, not frequency AI is inside the workflow — multi-step, multi-tool — not a box they visit for a quick answer.
2
They built something A template, a pipeline, a spec format, an agent. They changed how the work is done, not just how fast.
3
Previously impossible output Named work that could not have existed before — a full prototype, a working app, a one-day synthesis.
4
They already share They hand their method to others unprompted. This is what makes them usable as a distribution channel.
What this is not: a ranking of the team, and not a statement that the other 30 are behind. All seven are self-rated Pro — but so are 6 others who did not describe a transferable method. The list is about what can be copied, not who is best.

05 Interview guide — the same questions, seven times

Seven interviews only add up to something if they answer the same questions. Everything below is fixed; the champion-specific questions live in each dossier above. 60 minutes, three blocks, notes go straight into layer ③.

🧩 Block A · The method
  1. Walk me through your last real piece of work, start to finish. Where does AI enter, and where does it stop?
  2. What do you check before you trust an output?
  3. What do you still refuse to hand to a model — and why?
  4. What took you longest to learn that you could now teach in ten minutes?
👥 Block B · Inside the UX team
  1. What does your week look like now that it didn't a year ago?
  2. What do you produce today that design simply didn't produce before?
  3. Who have you already taught this to — and what did they get stuck on?
  4. If the whole team copied one thing from you, what should it be? And what should they not copy?
🤝 Block C · Product & Development
  1. How has working with Development changed? What do they now expect from you?
  2. How has working with Product changed?
  3. Where does the friction actually sit today — and is it new, or just faster?
  4. What would you ask the other two functions to do differently?
One rule for the notes. Record the method, not the enthusiasm. "Uses AI for research" is not transferable; "runs Dovetail first, then asks Claude to contradict it, then reads three raw transcripts" is. If a note cannot be handed to another designer as an instruction, it isn't finished.
Anonymity note. Unlike the survey's open answers, these interviews are attributed — the champions are named on this page, and their practices are credited to them. That is intentional and requires their agreement: nothing from an interview is published here without the champion having seen it first.

✅ Do's & Don'ts Based on survey data + Champion experience
✅ Do
⚡Use AI for the production work — spec writing, synthesis, annotation, microcopy. These are the highest-ROI tasks.
📖Always read raw transcripts yourself. AI finds patterns — you find meaning. Never skip the human validation step.
✏️Treat AI output as a first draft. 65% of outputs need minor edits, 35% need significant rework. Budget time for review.
🔄Use multiple tools to validate each other. One AI's output checked by another catches errors neither would catch alone.
📤Share what works. If a prompt saved you 2 hours, add it to the library. Zero people currently share nothing — let's keep it that way.
🎯Give AI specific context. The more specific the prompt (product, user type, constraint), the less rework you'll need.
❌ Don't
🚫Don't use AI output as-is. 0% of the team does — and that's right. Always review before sharing externally.
🚫Don't let Dev or PM use AI to build UI before UX is involved. AI-generated UI without UX input creates irreversible design debt.
🚫Don't paste confidential customer data into public AI tools without checking the company AI data policy first.
🚫Don't use AI to skip the thinking. Use it to compress production time. Judgment, prioritization, and decision-making stay human.
🚫Don't work in isolation. The biggest waste right now is everyone discovering the same things independently. Use the shared library.
🚫Don't ignore quality just because it was fast. Speed and quality aren't opposites — but AI can make it easy to miss problems at scale.
"AI eliminated production time. Thinking time stayed the same. What didn't compress: running sessions, prioritizing recommendations, reading transcripts yourself, making judgment calls." — Yaara Bar Feldman
