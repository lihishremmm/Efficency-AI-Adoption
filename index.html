The question We got faster. Did we get more efficient?

Those are not the same question. Velocity is how fast we complete a task we already knew how to do. Actual efficiency is what the team is left with once you account for everything that changed around it — a job that grew new categories of work, demand that rose, and a development organisation that also got faster. The first number is impressive. The second one is the one to plan with.

🧮 How both headline numbers are calculated

Q13–211.88× velocity. Every respondent sized the same four tasks twice — as they'd have sized it before AI, and as they'd size it today. Story-point bands are collapsed to their midpoints, weighted by how many people chose each band, and divided by 37.

Band midpoints: XS(1–2)=1.5 S(3–5)=4 M(5–8)=6.5 L(8–13)=10.5 XL(13–21)=17 Sum of all four scenarios, before AI: 215 + 298 + 224 + 246.5 = 983.5 SP Sum of all four scenarios, with AI: 106 + 156.5 + 123.5 + 138 = 524.0 SP Velocity = 983.5 ÷ 524.0 = 1.877 → 1.88× Effort/task = 524.0 ÷ 983.5 = 0.533 → −46.7% ("about 47% less effort")

Cross-check: averaging the four individual multipliers instead — (2.03 + 1.90 + 1.81 + 1.79) ÷ 4 = 1.883 — gives the same answer. Per-scenario workings are under the chart below.

Q13–21 × Q26−29% actual efficiency. Effort per task fell, but the number of tasks rose. Multiply the two:

Effort per task = 0.533 (from above) Tasks per designer = 1.34 (+34% — see leak 2 for this figure's derivation) Total effort today = 1.34 × 0.533 = 0.714 → −28.6% → −29%

CEILINGThis is the best case. It counts only work that existed before AI. Requests that had no pre-AI equivalent — prototypes, code — are additive on top and are not subtracted here, because no before-and-after measurement of them exists. See leak 1.

02 Where the velocity goes — three claims on the same dividend

A 1.88× speed-up is not 1.88× more free time. Three things absorb it, and only one of them is normally counted.

1

The four scenarios above compare like for like: tasks that existed before AI and still exist today. That is what makes them a fair measurement — and it is also what makes them incomplete. They cannot see work that had no "before".

That is a genuine expansion of the discipline's territory. It also means a meaningful share of the velocity dividend was spent the moment it was earned, on work that no before-and-after measurement will ever show.

🧮 How +34% is derived — and why it is the weakest number here

Q26"Since using AI, how has each of these changed?" — 37 respondents. The bars above are counts ÷ 37:

MetricIncreased (any)…of which "significantly"

The survey captured direction and intensity, but not magnitude — nobody was asked "by how much?". To get a usable number we assign values to the two intensity bands and weight by group size:

"a little" = 91.9% − 32.4% = 59.5% of the team ← assigned +25% "significantly" = 32.4% of the team ← assigned +60% Task increase = (0.595 × 0.25) + (0.324 × 0.60) = 0.1488 + 0.1944 = 0.343 → +34%

ESTIMATEThe +25% and +60% are our assumptions, not survey values. This is the only significant figure on the page that is not a direct response count. Everything downstream of it — the −29%, the 71 effort index, the ~40% headroom — inherits that uncertainty. Treat it as directional, never as a headline.

Sensitivity: at +15%/+40% the increase is +21% and total effort lands at 65 (−35%). At +35%/+80% it is +47% and effort lands at 78 (−22%). The conclusion — effort down, but far less than the speed-up suggests — holds across that whole range.

3
The benchmark moved too
Development is running at roughly the same multiplier we are

Efficiency is only ever relative to what everyone else is doing. 84% of the UX team believes R&D is now noticeably or significantly faster, which weights out to about 1.96× — almost exactly our own 1.88×.

We are at ~96% of Development's pace. The good news: the anxiety about UX falling behind is not supported by the data. The sobering news: doubling your speed while the organisation around you also doubles its speed leaves your relative position roughly where it started. Running to stand still still counts as running — but it does not feel like a gain.

NOT LIKE-FOR-LIKE1.88× comes from re-sizing four fixed tasks. 1.96× comes from one impression question, answered by people outside the team being rated. The two are not methodologically comparable and the 96% should be read as "roughly level", not as a precise ratio.

Caveat — and the obvious next step. The 1.96× is the UX team's perception of R&D, not R&D's own data, and the two figures aren't methodologically comparable. Running these same four scenario questions with the Development team would replace the softest number here with a hard one. It costs one survey.
The real friction isn't speed — it's sequence. AI made it cheap for Development and Product to produce something that looks finished before UX is involved. Once a stakeholder has seen a working screen, the conversation shifts from "what should this be?" to "can you tidy this up?" Among designers who have actually handed off AI-generated work, 59% hit moderate revision rounds or worse.

The team is doing ~34% more work for ~29% less total effort. There is roughly 40% more task headroom before total effort returns to pre-AI levels — a finite, one-time asset that is currently being spent by default rather than by decision.

🧮 The effort index and the "40% headroom" figure

Pre-AI total effort is indexed to 100. Each step multiplies the previous one:

Start 100 × effort per task (0.533) 53 ← 1.88× faster, so 1 ÷ 1.88 = 0.533 × more tasks (1.34) 71 ← 53 × 1.34 = 71.1 + new work categories ? ← additive, unmeasured

Headroom. Tasks could keep rising until total effort returns to 100:

Max tasks at pre-AI effort = 1 ÷ 0.533 = 1.876× the original volume Where the team is today = 1.34× the original volume Remaining room = 1.876 ÷ 1.34 = 1.40 → ~40% more tasks

INHERITS UNCERTAINTYBoth the 71 and the 40% depend on the +34% task figure, which is an estimate (see leak 2). The 53 is measured.

CEILINGThe "?" row is real work with no pre-AI baseline. It cannot be quantified from this survey, so the true effort index is 71 or higher and the true headroom is 40% or less.

🎯 What to say, and what not to say
✅ Defensible❌ Not defensible
"Effort per task fell about 47% — we're 1.88× faster at the work we already did." "The team is twice as efficient." We're twice as fast at old tasks, which is a different claim.
"Net total effort is down ~29%, and part of that headroom is already committed." "There's spare capacity, give us more." The 29% is what's left after demand already grew a third.
"UX now delivers prototypes and code that weren't previously in scope." "AI hasn't really helped, we're just busier." Effort per task genuinely halved and quality went up.
"Design is running at roughly Development's AI-driven pace — measured on our side, perceived on theirs." Any speed-up of 3× or more. The measured range is 1.79–2.03×. Anyone who checks will find it.
The one-sentence version: the team got about twice as fast at the work it already had, then spent most of that gain on more work, on entirely new categories of work that never existed before, and on keeping pace with a development organisation that got faster at the same time — which is why a 1.88× speed-up feels like roughly nothing at all.
