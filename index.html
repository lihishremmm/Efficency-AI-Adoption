1. Executive Summary

The UX team has moved past experimentation: AI is now a daily part of how design work gets done. 97% of respondents use Claude, 95% use AI daily, and every single respondent (0% "beginner") rates themselves at least mid-level. Across four matched before/after task scenarios, effort dropped 44–51% — consistent enough to treat as a real velocity baseline rather than a few enthusiastic outliers.

The team also reports the gains are broad, not just "faster": 92% say they now take on more tasks, 89% say overall efficiency increased, and 87% say output quality increased. The open risk is that adoption has outpaced structure — 51% say the AI data-usage policy is only partially clear or unclear, 59% say output quality still isn't good enough to trust as-is, and usability testing / validation remains the one UX stage AI has barely touched (49% moderate-or-higher use vs. 75–89% for every other stage).

This report translates the survey into a Velocity/Quality KPI baseline and a structured adoption program with concrete action items, geared toward the stated goal of roughly doubling designer efficiency.

Speed — two independent measures agree: timing the same 4 task types before vs. with AI gives 1.79x–2.03x faster (avg. 1.88x, i.e. ~47% less effort). Asked directly how much faster R&D feels overall (Q27), the team's own weighted estimate is 1.96x. Both land at essentially the same place: roughly double the speed.

Demand — 92% of the team says the number of tasks they take on has grown since adopting AI (60% "a little," 32% "significantly"); 84% say the volume of output they produce has grown too. Converting those categories to rough percentages ("a little" ≈ +25%, "significantly" ≈ +60% — an estimate, not a direct survey answer) gives ~34% more tasks and ~36% more output per designer on average. Separately, self-reported time savings (Q11) average ~7 hours/person/week, or roughly 260 hours/week of freed capacity across the 37 respondents — capacity that shows up as more requests absorbed rather than idle time.

Caveat: the "faster" figure is grounded in directly measured before/after estimates. The "more requested" figure is self-reported task/volume growth — the percentage magnitude is an estimate from category midpoints, not a number the survey asked for directly, and should be read as directional, not exact.

3.3 Quality: more trust, but verification is still the norm

  • Review discipline held: nobody uses AI output as-is — 65% review and lightly edit it, 35% significantly rework it before using it.
  • Dev acceptance is mixed: only 27% report just "minor fixes," 32% get moderate revision rounds, 8% get heavy pushback, and 32% haven't handed AI-generated work to dev yet.
  • Self-reported quality is up: 87% report quality of their output increased since using AI (41% "significantly"), and only 5% report a decrease.
  • But trust is the top blocker: 59% still cite output quality as the biggest barrier to using AI more — the single most-cited barrier in the survey, ahead of tokens/access (54%) or time to learn (38%).

The Validate gap is an opportunity, not a limitation: separately, the team rated usability-testing analysis as 92% automatable (19% fully, 73% partially) — nearly as automatable as design specs (92%) or research synthesis (95%) — yet it's the stage where AI is least actually used. Closing that gap is one of the highest-leverage moves available.

Workweek allocation (Q10) also shows design work now consumes "most of the week" (50%+) for 43% of respondents — more than any other activity, including meetings (16%) — suggesting AI has freed time for other categories (documentation, task management) while design itself remains the core time sink worth optimizing further.

3.5 What the team expects AI to produce

Top purposes for using AI: research & desk research (78%) and design ideation/wireframing (78%), followed by prototyping (62%), data synthesis (51%), writing/UX copy (43%), and documentation/handoff (38%). Expected outputs skew toward substance over polish: written docs/briefs (95%), interactive prototypes (89%), and design specs (86%) are expected far more often than slide decks (62%) or static Figma files (62%) — the team wants AI to produce artifacts that move work forward, not just visuals.

3.7 Concerns to address openly

  • In order of frequency: becoming too dependent on AI (70%), unsure whether AI output meets quality/accuracy standards (62%), data privacy/confidentiality (41%), AI replacing parts of their role (38%), and feeling pressure to use AI even when it's not the right tool (30%). Only 14% report no significant concerns.

These concerns should be named directly in any team-wide rollout rather than glossed over — the "dependency" and "pressure to use it" concerns in particular suggest some fatigue alongside the enthusiasm.

5. Recommended Program: Structured AI Adoption for the UX Team

Goal: increase design quality and team efficiency through practical AI adoption, targeting materially higher designer velocity while holding or raising quality. Four pillars, built directly from the findings above:

5.1 Capture & spread what's already working

  • Shortlist a few high-signal "Pro" users (heavy AI use, minimal rework needed, already sharing workflows) and run short interviews; convert the best workflows into 3–5 shared Claude project templates / prompt patterns for the highest-volume tasks (research synthesis, spec handoff, wireframing).
  • Stand up the "curated prompt library for UX tasks" 51% of the team asked for, seeded from those interviews.

5.2 Close the Validate/usability-testing gap

  • Usability testing is rated 92% automatable but only 49% actually uses AI at moderate-or-higher levels — the single biggest efficiency headroom identified in the survey. Pilot AI-assisted usability synthesis with 2–3 volunteers and share results.

5.3 Build quality trust, not just speed

  • Since 59% still don't trust output quality and 35% significantly rework AI output, define a lightweight review checklist for AI-generated specs/research before handoff, to reduce dev pushback (currently 40% moderate-or-heavy).
  • Track the dev-pushback and quality-barrier metrics from Section 4 each quarter as leading indicators of trust, not just usage.

5.4 Remove structural friction

  • Clarify the AI data-usage policy — only 49% currently find it clear; this is a quick, low-cost fix with outsized trust impact.
  • Run the hands-on workshops and protected "experiment time" 70% of the team asked for, rather than adding more passive documentation.
  • Revisit token/tool-budget constraints (cited by 54% and 24% respectively) alongside Finance/IT, using the time-savings data in Section 3.2 to justify spend.
